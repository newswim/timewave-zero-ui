About the Timewave

what this is, where it came from, and how it was rebuilt

The idea

Terence McKenna (1946–2000) believed that time has a structure. In his account, history moves between two conditions he called habit and novelty, and the balance between them follows a fixed curve. He derived that curve from the King Wen sequence, the traditional ordering of the 64 hexagrams of the I Ching, and claimed it repeated at every scale of time, from billions of years down to minutes. The curve reaches zero on December 21, 2012. At that point, he said, novelty would become infinite and history as we know it would end.[5]

He called the model Timewave Zero. It began after an experience in the Colombian Amazon in 1971, took written form in The Invisible Landscape (1975, with his brother Dennis), and became computer software in the 1980s, principally written by Peter Meyer.[3] McKenna presented the wave in lectures for the rest of his life.

The history, briefly

The dates that matter: the La Chorrera experiment, 1971. The Invisible Landscape, 1975. First graphical software on an Apple II+, around 1978; Meyer's DOS program, 1989–91; his Fractal Time, 1998.[3] In 1996 the mathematician Matthew Watkins showed that a step in the original construction, the so-called half twist, did not do what the theory's documentation claimed. McKenna acknowledged the problem and published Watkins' critique himself.[6] A physicist, John Sheliak, produced a corrected construction in 1998, which McKenna adopted.[8] McKenna died of brain cancer in April 2000. December 21, 2012 passed without incident.

The mathematics

The wave is built in three steps, and all three are reproduced in this site's source code, where automated tests check them against the output of Meyer's original program on every change.

1. From the hexagrams to 64 numbers. Take the 64 hexagrams in King Wen order and count how many of the six lines change at each step from one hexagram to the next, wrapping around at the end. The result is this sequence: 

6 2 4 4 4 3 2 4 2 4 6 2 2 4 2 2 6 3 4 3 2 2 2 3 4 2 6 2 6 3 2 3
4 4 4 2 4 6 4 3 2 4 2 3 4 3 2 3 4 4 4 1 6 2 2 3 4 3 2 1 6 3 6 3

This sequence has real structure. Exactly three quarters of the values are even. The value 5 never appears. McKenna noticed these properties and estimated, by generating random alternatives, that an ordering this regular occurs about once in several thousand tries. We repeated his experiment with a million trials; under the strictest reading his estimate holds up within its own margin of error.[12]

2. From 64 numbers to 384. A geometric procedure expands the sequence: it is overlaid with a reversed copy of itself, then combined at three scales (single lines, trigrams, whole hexagrams), and the result is measured at 384 points. Watkins compressed the whole procedure into one formula.[6] [7] In simplified form, for each position k from 0 to 383: 

w[k] = |skew(k)| + |divergence(k)|

skew(k)       combines line, trigram, and hexagram terms of the
              form h[k-1] - h[k-2] + h[-k] - h[1-k], the trigram
              term scaled by 3, the hexagram term by 6; the
              disputed "half twist" is a set of alternating signs
              applied here
divergence(k) combines terms of the form 9 - h[-k] - h[k-1],
              scaled the same way

Run without the twist, the formula reproduces the 1996 "Watkins" data set exactly, all 384 values. Run with the twist, it reproduces the original 1975 "Kelley" set at 383 of 384 values. The one exception is position 119, where every historical copy reads 22 and the formula gives 32. No published source mentions this discrepancy; we found it while rebuilding the pipeline, and we preserve the historical value while documenting the difference.[12] Sheliak's 1998 construction, recovered from his paper, matches his data set at 382 of 384 values, with similar small unexplained differences at two points.

3. From 384 numbers to a wave over all of time. The 384 values are treated as one cycle of a waveform, and cycles are nested inside each other at a ratio of 64: one cycle spans 384 days, the next 67.29 years, the next 4,306 years, and so on past the age of the universe. The value at any moment is the sum over all these scales: 

f(x) = [ sum over scales i where 64^i ≤ x of  64^i · v(x / 64^i)
       + sum over sub-day scales of        v(x · 64^i) / 64^i ] / 64³

x = days before the zero date; v = the 384 values, joined by
straight lines and repeated

Because every data set begins 0, 0, 0, the first sum ends on its own and the wave touches zero exactly once, at the end. The same property makes the wave exactly self-similar: f(64x) = 64·f(x). That identity is what the ⇑64 button demonstrates, and it is the one claim of the theory that is simply true.[1]

The zero date is not produced by any of this. The wave's shape fixes no endpoint. McKenna first anchored the end by placing Hiroshima at the opening of the final 67.29-year cycle, which pointed at November 2012; he later moved the date to December 21 to match the Maya calendar's long-count ending.[4] Meyer, who wrote the software, reached the same conclusion we did: the theory never derived its own end date.

What we make of it

As a prediction, the timewave failed on schedule. As mathematics, it is real and rather elegant. As a cultural object, it mattered: it is a root of the whole 2012 phenomenon, and it occupied one of the more original minds of the late twentieth century for thirty years. We restored it the way a museum restores an orrery: the mechanism runs, the label tells the truth. Every number on this site can be traced to a source, and the parts of the story that are wrong are said to be wrong.

How this site was made

This site was built by Dan Minshew with substantial help from an AI assistant (Anthropic's Claude), which contributed research, code, and draft text under human direction and review. The mathematics is not AI-generated: the wave algorithm is Peter Meyer's, ported from his public-domain C code and verified against his compiled program at about 1,900 reference points by an automated test suite that runs on every change. The historical claims above are cited to their sources below. Corrections are welcome at the project's issue tracker.
